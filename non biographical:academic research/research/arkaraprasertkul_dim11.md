## Non Arkaraprasertkul — Research Impact Analysis

## Executive Summary

Non Arkaraprasertkul's research represents a distinctive methodological and theoretical contribution to urban studies, gentrification theory, and heritage studies. Anchored in long-term ethnographic fieldwork (2013–2015 in a Shanghai lilong neighborhood, with broader engagement spanning 2007–2025+), his work advances an anthropological perspective that challenges the dominant displacement-centric narrative in gentrification scholarship. His core theoretical innovation — the concept of **"gentrification from within"** — reframes original residents as active agents who strategically mobilize heritage as cultural capital, achieving upward social mobility without government or developer intervention. This analysis documents the methodological distinctiveness, theoretical contributions, and scholarly reception of his work, while critically assessing its strengths and limitations.

**Key Findings:**
- **Methodological innovation:** Two-year immersive ethnography in a single lilong neighborhood, combining participant observation with architectural expertise [^24^]
- **Core theoretical contribution:** "Gentrification from within" — a framework that positions original residents as heritage entrepreneurs rather than passive victims [^259^]
- **Heritage-gentrification nexus:** Heritage is conceptualized as an economic resource and driver of gentrification, not merely its victim [^23^]
- **Scholarly recognition:** Endorsed by leading scholars including Alan Smart (anthropologist), Fulong Wu (geographer), and Michael Herzfeld (dissertation advisor) [^27^] [^259^]
- **Geographic focus:** Deep Shanghai case studies that contribute to comparative urbanism debates about Global South gentrification [^458^]

## 1. Methodological Profile: Ethnography as Urban Research Method

### 1.1 Ethnographic Research Design

Arkaraprasertkul's methodology is explicitly ethnographic. His 2018 paper in *Urban Studies* is described as "Based on ethnographic research during 2013–2015" [^24^]. The methodology extends beyond standard interviews to encompass what he describes as a "full-time job" of participant observation:

> "We anthropologists do what we call 'ethnography,' which to me, is a full-time job. Ethnography consists of several qualitative research methods, aiming at collecting the empirical data on human societies and cultures. One of the main methods is what we call 'participant observations,' which is basically how we anthropologists become a part of the community we study in order to learn about the culture of the people we research." [^172^]

His daily research routine included systematic observation of community dynamics, cooking and eating patterns, corridor interactions, and recording of oral histories — what might be termed an architectural-anthropological hybrid methodology that leverages his dual training.

### 1.2 Distinctiveness in the Methodological Landscape

The gentrification literature is characterized by a persistent methodological dichotomy between quantitative "macro" analyses (census-based, GIS-mapped) and qualitative "micro" case studies [^447^]. Sideris et al. (2017) describe this as a "disconnect" between approaches that gather and analyze different types of data, with few studies adopting mixed methods [^447^].

Recent reviews of gentrification methodology highlight this tension:

> "A methodological dichotomy characterizes much of the existing gentrification literature, as studies are either quantitative, 'macro' analyses or qualitative, 'micro' inquiries of neighborhoods in the form of case studies... But there is often a 'disconnect' between quantitative and qualitative approaches." [^447^]

Arkaraprasertkul's ethnographic approach is positioned at the qualitative, micro end of this spectrum, but with notable depth. His two-year fieldwork commitment exceeds the typical duration of qualitative gentrification studies, which often focus on "a single neighborhood or small group of neighborhoods" over shorter periods [^447^].

Alan Smart, in his 2018 review in *Urban Studies*, specifically praised the methodological rigor:

> "The argument is all the more persuasive because of the rich ethnographic material, based on two years of fieldwork, that he provides." [^27^]

### 1.3 Anthropological Distinctiveness

The anthropological perspective on gentrification remains relatively rare in urban studies. As Herzfeld (2010) argues, anthropologists' tendency to focus on particular communities rather than generalized social processes "may be its greatest strength" in the context of globalized urban restructuring [^286^].

Arkaraprasertkul's anthropological training — his PhD advisor was Michael Herzfeld at Harvard, a leading figure in the anthropology of historic conservation and gentrification [^259^] [^401^] — positions his work within a specific intellectual tradition that emphasizes:

- **Ethnographies of the particular** over generalized social processes [^286^]
- **Long-term field engagement** over short research visits
- **Resident agency and everyday practice** over structural determinism
- **The politics of built form** as lived experience

## 2. Core Theoretical Contribution: "Gentrification from Within"

### 2.1 The Concept

Arkaraprasertkul's signature theoretical contribution is the concept of **"gentrification from within,"** first articulated in his 2016 article in *Asian Anthropology* (Vol. 15/16, Issue 1, pp. 1–20) [^37^] [^29^] and further developed in his 2018 *Urban Studies* paper. This concept describes a process whereby original working-class residents actively capitalize on heritage value to become economic beneficiaries of neighborhood change, rather than its victims.

The dissertation abstract provides the clearest formulation:

> "In 'Chapter Five: Aspect of Change,' I propose the concept of 'gentrification from within' to explain the phenomenon whereby communities become more diversified. Unlike in most places where gentrification has had a rather negative impact by depriving low-income residents of their homes, the process of 'gentrification from within' has had the reverse effect of making the [neighborhood more diverse]." [^259^]

### 2.2 Theoretical Distinctiveness

The concept directly challenges the displacement-only narrative that dominates gentrification studies. As the 2018 paper states:

> "By suggesting an alternative process of gentrification in which not all residents are displaced unwillingly, this paper shows that the idea of gentrification demands more attention." [^24^]

This theoretical move is significant because, as Brown-Saracino (2017) notes, gentrification scholarship is divided between:
- **Quantitative researchers** who find displacement is "far from endemic" in gentrifying areas
- **Qualitative scholars** who paint a "dire picture" of displacement and cultural change [^470^]

Arkaraprasertkul's framework offers a third position: displacement is not the only or inevitable outcome, and original residents can be active agents in the process.

## 3. Heritage as Driver of Gentrification: Theoretical Innovation

### 3.1 Reframing Heritage

Arkaraprasertkul's second major theoretical contribution lies in reconceptualizing the relationship between heritage and gentrification. Rather than treating heritage as a victim of gentrification (the conventional view), he positions heritage as an **active driver** and **economic resource** that original residents strategically mobilize.

> "I argue that the high level of flexibility of the notion of heritage enables a form of gentrification that differs from classic gentrification whereby middle-class residents push the working class out of a neighbourhood. The original working-class residents who understand the value system of the middle class are those profiting thanks to their ability to 'sell' the old lilong buildings as historically important and ultimately as middle-class heritage." [^23^]

This framework draws on Bourdieu's concept of cultural capital — the idea that cultural knowledge and taste function as forms of social currency [^464^]. Arkaraprasertkul shows how original residents, understanding the middle-class value system, convert their housing stock (lilong buildings) into culturally valuable heritage commodities.

## 4. Challenging the Displacement Narrative

### 4.1 The Displacement Debate

The gentrification literature is deeply divided on the question of displacement. As the Urban Institute's gentrification guide notes:

> "Brown-Saracino (2017) reviews the state of the gentrification and displacement debate and finds two opposing camps, informed to a large degree by different research approaches... quantitative researchers generally find that displacement is 'far from endemic' in gentrifying places... Qualitative scholars, on the other hand, have painted a 'dire picture' of gentrification." [^470^]

Arkaraprasertkul's work enters this debate from a unique angle: he documents cases where **original residents are not displaced** but instead become active economic beneficiaries. His findings show:

- Original residents "mobilise their knowledge of this particular selling point to benefit themselves economically by becoming renters" [^24^]
- The process produces "upward social mobility without any intervention by the local government or real estate developers" [^24^]
- "Not all residents are displaced unwillingly" [^24^]

### 4.2 Comparison with Related Concepts

The concept of "gentrification without displacement" has emerged in the literature independently. Tsai (2025) published a paper titled "Gentrification without displacement? A case study of heritage-led development in post-socialist China" [^424^] [^425^], demonstrating that Arkaraprasertkul's framework has opened space for new research questions. However, Arkaraprasertkul's "gentrification from within" is more theoretically precise, emphasizing not merely the absence of displacement but the **active agency of original residents** in driving the process.

Scholars of working-class gentrification have also documented resident agency, though from different angles:

> "Working-class residents participate in gentrification by negotiating and consuming the process on their own terms, for their own gains. This negotiation also reveals the fallacy of theorisations which position agency outside of structure." [^451^]

Arkaraprasertkul's contribution is to document this agency in the specific context of heritage commodification in a Chinese metropolis, where the state-land ownership system and rapid urbanization create unique conditions.

## 5. Comparative Urbanism Contribution

### 5.1 Shanghai as a Theoretical Laboratory

Arkaraprasertkul's Shanghai-focused research contributes to what Lees, Shin, and Lopez-Morales (2016) call **"planetary gentrification"** — the recognition that gentrification is a global urban strategy that manifests differently across contexts [^458^] [^459^]. His work is particularly valuable because:

1. **It challenges Northern-centric models**: Classic gentrification theory (Glass 1964; Smith 1979; Ley 1980) was developed in London, New York, and Canadian cities. Arkaraprasertkul shows how Shanghai's state-land ownership system, rapid urbanization, and heritage politics produce a "different form of gentrification that departs from classic gentrification models" [^23^].

2. **It documents endogenous processes**: Unlike much Global South gentrification research that emphasizes state-led or externally-driven development, Arkaraprasertkul documents a **resident-driven** process that occurs "without any intervention by the local government or real estate developers" [^24^].

3. **It speaks to comparative urbanism debates**: Following Jennifer Robinson's call for "ordinary cities" approaches that decenter Northern urban theory [^458^], Arkaraprasertkul's work provides the kind of "deep" and "case-oriented empirical lens" that comparative urbanism requires [^458^].

### 5.2 The Shanghai Focus: Depth vs. Generalizability

The geographic concentration on Shanghai raises methodological questions. As one review of Chinese gentrification studies noted:

> "The cases are mainly focused on studying the 1st tier cities — Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Guangzhou in China... The reason behind this spatial pattern of gentrification studies could be that the researchers are mainly located in the 1st and 2nd tiers cities, where most of the recognized universities and academic resources are concentrated." [^167^]

However, this geographic limitation is offset by significant **methodological depth**. Arkaraprasertkul's sustained engagement with Shanghai (first visit 2006, continuous research through 2025+) [^172^] provides the kind of longitudinal, embedded knowledge that compensates for geographic narrowness:

> "I have been coming back to Shanghai every year, except in 2010, to study them!" [^172^]

The depth of his Shanghai knowledge — what he describes as learning "from those I meant to learn from" over "the last decade of my life" [^259^] — enables the theoretical claims about gentrification from within that a more comparative but shallower approach could not support.

## 7. Theoretical Frameworks and Models

### 7.1 "Original Residents as Active Actors"

Arkaraprasertkul's theoretical framework centers on resident agency. The 2018 paper explicitly:

> "[P]resents a case exemplifying a process of social change in which the 'original residents' themselves are active actors." [^24^]

This framework builds on but extends existing agency-centered gentrification scholarship. While researchers like Tom Slater have critiqued the "eviction of critical perspectives from gentrification research" [^348^], and working-class gentrification scholars have documented resident negotiation [^451^], Arkaraprasertkul's contribution is to show original residents not merely surviving or negotiating gentrification, but **actively driving and benefiting from it** through heritage commodification.

### 7.2 Cultural Capital and Urban Uniqueness

The framework mobilizes Bourdieu's concept of cultural capital in a novel urban context:

> "[U]npacking how the notion architectural uniqueness of an urban heritage neighbourhood has imbued itself with cultural capital in the eyes of the new residents." [^24^]

This theoretical move connects gentrification studies with:
- Urban heritage studies (how built environment acquires cultural value)
- Economic anthropology (how cultural knowledge becomes economic resource)
- Comparative urbanism (how cultural capital operates differently in Chinese vs. Western contexts)

### 7.3 The Politics of Built Form

Arkaraprasertkul's architectural training (MIT master's) informs his analysis of how the **physical form** of lilong houses — their spatial configuration, shared facilities, courtyard structures — shapes social relations and economic possibilities. This architectural-anthropological synthesis represents a methodological innovation that few gentrification scholars can replicate:

> "It was he [Michael Herzfeld] who had initially provided me with the argument that architecture and anthropology could benefit from each other." [^259^]

## 8. Contribution to Comparative Urbanism

### 8.1 The Global South Question

Arkaraprasertkul's work speaks directly to debates about whether gentrification theory, developed in the Global North, can be applied to the Global South. As Lees (2012, 2014) has argued, urban theory has been "colonial, hegemonic, and based on a selective number of cities" [^458^].

Arkaraprasertkul's contribution is to:

1. **Document a non-Western variant of gentrification** that does not fit Northern models
2. **Show how local context** (state land ownership, heritage politics, Shanghai's treaty-port history) shapes gentrification outcomes
3. **Provide empirical depth** that enables genuine comparison rather than theoretical imposition

The "gentrification from within" concept has been applied to other Asian contexts — for example, Yogyakarta, Indonesia, where researchers have drawn parallels with "internal gentrification processes observed in Hong Kong's lilong areas" [^160^].

### 8.2 State-Market-Resident Dynamics

Arkaraprasertkul's work enriches the comparative urbanism literature by documenting a **three-way dynamic** between state, market, and residents that differs from Western cases:

> "By investigating how residents, planners, and local officials variously conceive of historic preservation and urban renewal programs, and by eschewing the artificially coherent image of the city promoted by state planners, I paint a more nuanced picture of the specific challenges faced by the populace and their creative methods of negotiation, adaptation, and [survival]." [^370^]

This approach aligns with calls for comparative urbanism that attend to "the nuanced exercise of state hegemony" [^423^] and "the expropriation of public land and housing for gentrification" that characterizes Global South contexts [^459^].

## 9. Critical Assessment: Strengths and Limitations

### 9.1 Strengths

1. **Methodological depth**: Two years of immersive fieldwork exceeds typical gentrification case studies
2. **Theoretical originality**: "Gentrification from within" is a genuinely new conceptual contribution
3. **Interdisciplinary synthesis**: Bridges architecture, anthropology, and urban studies
4. **Longitudinal engagement**: 18+ years of Shanghai research provides deep contextual knowledge
5. **Scholarly recognition**: Endorsed by leading figures across multiple disciplines

### 9.2 Limitations

1. **Geographic scope**: Single-city focus limits generalizability; acknowledged in literature as a pattern in Chinese gentrification research [^167^]
2. **Single-neighborhood depth**: The ethnography focuses on one lilong compound, raising questions about representativeness
3. **Qualitative methodology limits**: As noted in methodological reviews, qualitative approaches "typically focus on the portrayal and description of small groups and personal experiences, which may not fully reflect the diverse needs of broader social groups" [^446^]
4. **Absence of quantitative triangulation**: Unlike mixed-methods approaches that combine ethnography with census data verification [^447^], Arkaraprasertkul's work relies primarily on qualitative data
5. **Temporal specificity**: The 2013–2015 fieldwork captures a specific moment in Shanghai's rapid urban transformation; the dynamics may have shifted since

## 10. Conclusions

Non Arkaraprasertkul's research makes a **significant methodological and theoretical contribution** to urban studies, gentrification theory, and heritage studies. His core innovations include:

1. **"Gentrification from within"**: A theoretical framework that repositions original residents as active heritage entrepreneurs rather than passive victims of displacement

2. **Heritage as economic driver**: A reconceptualization of heritage from gentrification's victim to its catalyst and resource

3. **Anthropological-ethnographic methodology**: Two-year immersive fieldwork that provides the empirical depth necessary to support counter-intuitive theoretical claims

4. **Architectural-anthropological synthesis**: A unique interdisciplinary approach combining built environment expertise with social analysis

The work has been recognized by leading scholars (Alan Smart, Fulong Wu, Michael Herzfeld) and is cited across gentrification studies, heritage studies, and urban anthropology literatures. While the geographic focus on Shanghai and the single-neighborhood ethnographic approach limit generalizability, the theoretical framework of "gentrification from within" has proven portable enough to be applied in other Asian contexts. The work contributes meaningfully to comparative urbanism debates by documenting how gentrification operates differently under Chinese conditions of state land ownership, rapid urbanization, and heritage politics — providing empirical depth that challenges Northern-centric theoretical hegemony.
