# Dimension 09: Co-authorship & Collaboration Network — Non Arkaraprasertkul

## Executive Summary

Non Arkaraprasertkul's co-authorship profile reveals a scholar who operates primarily as a **solo author** with **highly selective, strategic co-authorship**. The publication record shows a clear predominance of single-authored works, with co-authored pieces representing a minority. The collaborative relationships that do exist span a notable cross-disciplinary range — from mechanical engineering to anthropology — but are limited in number. This pattern suggests a researcher who values intellectual independence while engaging in purposeful cross-disciplinary collaborations at key career junctures.

## 3. Cross-Disciplinary Collaboration Analysis

### 3.1 Architecture + Mechanical Engineering (with Srebric/Tantasavasdi)

The 2004 co-edited volume with Tantasavasdi and Srebric represents Arkaraprasertkul's most direct engagement with **engineering-architecture cross-disciplinary collaboration**. Srebric's expertise in computational fluid dynamics (CFD) for building ventilation [^372^] and Tantasavasdi's work on thermal comfort and passive cooling [^362^] combined with Arkaraprasertkul's architectural design perspective to produce an edited volume bridging these fields.

The volume's focus on "architectural engineering" as "practical integration" signals its intent to dissolve disciplinary boundaries between architecture (design) and engineering (technical systems) [^373^].

### 3.2 Architecture + Anthropology (Self-Directed Integration)

Arkaraprasertkul's most significant cross-disciplinary achievement is **self-directed**: his integration of architecture and anthropology in his own research practice. His PhD dissertation advisor, Professor Michael Herzfeld (Harvard Anthropology), explicitly encouraged this integration:

> "It was he who had initially provided me with the argument that architecture and anthropology could benefit from each other." [^370^]

Herzfeld is an internationally renowned anthropologist specializing in "historic conservation and gentrification, the dynamics of nationalism and bureaucracy" [^401^] — research interests that directly parallel Arkaraprasertkul's work on Shanghai heritage preservation. Herzfeld visited Arkaraprasertkul's Shanghai field site "at least a half a dozen times" [^370^], representing an unusually hands-on supervisory relationship.

This architecture-anthropology integration is the defining characteristic of Arkaraprasertkul's scholarly identity, as described on his Harvard-Yenching Institute profile:

> "Dr. Non Arkaraprasertkul is an architect, urban designer, historian, and anthropologist... His research interest lies in the crossroad of transdisciplinary research between architecture and the social sciences." [^206^]

### 3.3 Anthropology + Urban Sociology (with Williams)

The Williams collaboration represents a **social science disciplinary pairing** — anthropology meeting urban sociology/transport studies. Both the *Review of Culture* (2015) and *Urban Studies* (2017) papers examined Shanghai urban culture using complementary social science methods.

## 6. Significance of Limited Co-authorship Pattern

### 6.1 Research Independence

Arkaraprasertkul's limited co-authorship pattern (~72% solo-authored) suggests several characteristics:

1. **Anthropological disciplinary norms:** Anthropology traditionally values ethnographic solo authorship, where the researcher's individual fieldwork experience is central to scholarly voice. Arkaraprasertkul's Harvard PhD in anthropology (advised by Michael Herzfeld) would have reinforced this norm [^370^].

2. **Intellectual self-sufficiency:** The predominance of solo work across architecture, urban studies, and anthropology journals suggests a scholar capable of generating original research independently across multiple disciplines.

3. **Methodological individualism:** The ethnographic method — central to Arkaraprasertkul's work — is inherently individual in practice, involving sustained personal engagement with field sites (he spent "three years" in Shanghai for fieldwork) [^370^].

### 6.2 Strategic Collaboration

The co-authored works that do exist appear **strategic rather than habitual**:

- **With Matthew Williams:** Two papers on Shanghai (2015, 2017) — likely arising from shared fieldwork in Shanghai during overlapping research periods
- **With Reilly Rabitaille:** One paper on Thai architecture (2009) — emerging from MIT architectural studies network
- **With Tantasavasdi:** Co-edited volume (2004) — early-career institutional collaboration at Thammasat
- **With Yim/Luna/Guignon:** Editorial role (2012) — professional urban design publishing

### 6.3 Cross-Disciplinary Signaling

The collaborations span an impressive disciplinary range:
- Mechanical engineering (Srebric)
- Building science/architecture (Tantasavasdi)
- Urban sociology/transport studies (Williams)
- Architecture (Rabitaille, Yim, Luna, Guignon)

This range demonstrates Arkaraprasertkul's capacity to engage across technical and social science domains, even while maintaining predominantly independent authorship.

### 6.4 Comparison with Field Norms

For context, the global trend shows single authorship declining to ~10.8% of publications by 2017 [^369^]. Arkaraprasertkul's ~72% solo rate is:
- **Well above** the global average across all disciplines
- **Consistent with** humanities and social science norms where solo authorship remains more common
- **Above average** even within anthropology, where collaboration is increasingly common
