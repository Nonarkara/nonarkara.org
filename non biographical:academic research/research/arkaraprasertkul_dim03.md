# Dimension 03: Top Publications Deep Dive — Non Arkaraprasertkul

## Executive Summary

This report provides an in-depth analysis of the five most-cited publications by Non Arkaraprasertkul, an architect, urban designer, historian, and anthropologist trained at MIT and Harvard. His work focuses on Shanghai's urban transformation, particularly gentrification, heritage preservation, and lilong (alleyway) housing. These five papers collectively represent 269 citations and establish Arkaraprasertkul as a leading voice in the anthropology of urban China, with his concept of "gentrification from within" recognized as a significant theoretical contribution to gentrification studies globally.

---

## Publication 1: "Gentrification and its contentment" (2018) — 87 citations

### Bibliographic Details
- **Title:** Gentrification and its contentment: An anthropological perspective on housing, heritage and urban social change in Shanghai
- **Journal:** Urban Studies, 55(7), 1561-1578
- **DOI:** 10.1177/0042098016684313
- **Citations:** 87 (Google Scholar)

### Full Abstract
> "Based on ethnographic research during 2013-2015, this study describes an alternative form of gentrification in a traditional urban neighbourhood in Shanghai, unpacking how the notion architectural uniqueness of an urban heritage neighbourhood has imbued itself with cultural capital in the eyes of the new residents. By understanding how the original residents mobilise their knowledge of this particular selling point to benefit themselves economically by becoming renters, this study presents a case exemplifying a process of social change in which the 'original residents' themselves are active actors. The results of this process are the socioeconomic and ethic diversification of the neighbourhood as well as upward social mobility without any intervention by the local government or real estate developers. By suggesting an alternative process of gentrification in which not all residents are displaced unwillingly, this paper shows that the idea of gentrification demands more attention." [^1^]

### Key Arguments
1. **Alternative gentrification model:** Original residents are not passive victims but active economic agents who become renters and profit from heritage value
2. **Cultural capital mobilization:** Architectural uniqueness of heritage neighborhoods becomes a resource that original residents learn to monetize
3. **No government/developer intervention:** Unlike classic gentrification, this process occurs through resident agency alone
4. **Socioeconomic diversification without displacement:** The neighborhood achieves ethnic and class diversification while original residents experience upward mobility

### Notable Scholarly Reception
- **Alan Smart (University of Calgary):** Praised the paper as "an exploration of a kind of gentrification that is generally neglected in most of the literature," adding that "the argument is all the more persuasive because of the rich ethnographic material, based on two years of fieldwork, that he provides" [^2^]
- **Fulong Wu (UCL):** Cited the paper in *Urban Studies* to illustrate how "dwellers of the old urban area of Shanghai seized the opportunity of a rising demand for rental housing in central areas and upgraded their properties" [^2^]
- The paper appears in systematic reviews of gentrification in China, positioned alongside the work of He Shenjing and Wu Fulong [^3^]
- Cited by researchers studying heritage-led rural gentrification in China [^4^]

### Conceptual Contribution
This paper is Arkaraprasertkul's most impactful work. It challenges the displacement-centric narrative of gentrification theory by demonstrating a case where original working-class residents strategically deploy heritage discourse to their economic advantage. The concept of "contentment" reframes gentrification as potentially desirable for some residents, complicating the victim narrative. **Confidence: High** that this is his most conceptually innovative paper.

## Publication 2: "Gentrifying heritage" (2019) — 63 citations

### Bibliographic Details
- **Title:** Gentrifying heritage: how historic preservation drives gentrification in urban Shanghai
- **Journal:** International Journal of Heritage Studies, 25(9), 882-896
- **DOI:** 10.1080/13527258.2018.1460732
- **Citations:** 63 (Google Scholar); 29 CrossRef citations to date

### Full Abstract
> "In Shanghai, one can not only see, but live in the remnants of the city's semi-colonial legacy. These are the remaining lane houses known as lilong that are scattered throughout the city. Urbanites living in foreign settlements built them and resided there between the late nineteenth century to the mid 1940s. Confiscated by the Communist government upon its takeover in 1949, these buildings were redistributed to socialist workers and later on heavily altered to accommodate the massive influx of migrants during the first two decades of the opening up and reform era. The lilong houses are an important part of the memories of local Shanghainese residents. In this paper, I look primarily at the original residents' savviness in capitalising on heritage as a source of income and thereby using it as a defence against economic precariousness. I argue that the high level of flexibility of the notion of heritage enables a form of gentrification that differs from classic gentrification whereby middle-class residents push the working class out of a neighbourhood. The original working-class residents who understand the value system of the middle class are those profiting thanks to their ability to 'sell' the old lilong buildings as historically important and ultimately as middle-class heritage. This process, however, eventually produces a middle-class enclave similar to that of a classic gentrified neighbourhood. The remnants of the past such as those found in these traditional-looking neighbourhoods facilitate original residents' navigation of the abrupt social changes wrought by the market-driven urban Chinese economy of the past three decades." [^5^]

### Key Arguments
1. **Heritage as economic strategy:** Original working-class residents "sell" heritage significance to middle-class newcomers
2. **Flexibility of heritage concept:** The malleable notion of heritage enables working-class residents to reframe their properties as culturally valuable
3. **Working-class agency:** Residents possess "savviness" to capitalize on heritage as income and defense against precarity
4. **Eventual middle-class enclave:** Despite original resident agency, the outcome structurally resembles classic gentrification

### Notable Scholarly Reception
- Published in a special issue on "Heritage, Gentrification, Participation" co-edited by Chiara De Cesari and Rozita Dimova [^6^]
- Cited in systematic reviews of urban gentrification in mainland China [^7^]
- Referenced in the book *Heritage, Gentrification and Resistance in the Neoliberal City* (Berghahn Books) [^8^]
- Cited in research on heritage-led rural gentrification in China [^4^]
- Referenced in architectural heritage conservation frameworks at Delft University of Technology [^9^]
- Cited in studies of causal theories for urban upgrades in historic city centres [^10^]

### Conceptual Contribution
This paper develops the theoretical link between heritage preservation and gentrification, arguing that heritage designation is not merely a top-down imposition but can be a tool deployed by working-class residents themselves. The concept of "heritage flexibility" has become important in heritage studies. **Confidence: High**.

## Publication 3: "Towards modern urban housing" (2009) — 50 citations

### Bibliographic Details
- **Title:** Towards modern urban housing: redefining Shanghai's lilong
- **Journal:** Journal of Urbanism: International Research on Placemaking and Urban Sustainability, 2(1), 11-29
- **DOI:** 10.1080/17549170902833816
- **Citations:** 50 (Google Scholar)

### Full Abstract
> "This paper examines both the traditional and the modern aspects of the lilong, traditional low-rise row houses adapted from the Western tradition to accommodate the families of Chinese workers from the beginning of the Treaty Port era in the late 19th century to the establishment of the People's Republic in 1948. The paper aims to redefine the abstract concept of the lilong, arguing for their potential to be rethought as a typology of low-medium rise-high density (LMRHD) housing today. In particular, the paper delivers a practical answer to a conceptual question: how does lilong provide the dwelling identity of Shanghai, China, taking into account its form, meaning, and culture? The emergence of both lilong and Western modern housing is rooted in a crisis of space and the economic drive of modern cities. Lilong architecture and the normative living programme embedded in the typology of modern housing have been proper development housing strategies in modern Shanghai. By closely examining both the physical and community aspects that make lilong a mediating agency between Chinese locality and Western modernity, the paper presents the assumption that the architecture of lilong does not confine itself to certain forms or physical configurations; instead it is an 'abstract concept' of an urban neighbourhood – the spatial organization, architectural practicality, casual formation of semi-private space, and community lane-life – a concept that should be taken into account for the design of urban housing today." [^11^]

### Key Arguments
1. **Lilong as abstract concept:** The lilong is not merely a physical housing type but an "abstract concept" of urban neighborhood life
2. **LMRHD typology potential:** Lilong can be rethought as a model for low-medium rise-high density housing relevant to contemporary urban design
3. **Dwelling identity of Shanghai:** The lilong embodies Shanghai's unique identity through its form, meaning, and culture
4. **Mediating agency:** Lilong mediates between Chinese locality and Western modernity

### Notable Scholarly Reception
- Described in a University of Georgia thesis as one of "only a few researches (Arkaraprasertkul 2009; Lange 2015) [that] proposed a potential model for a future lilong" [^12^]
- Cited in a Politecnico di Milano thesis on contemporary interpretation of lilong spatial and social organization [^13^]
- Referenced in Delft University of Technology heritage conservation frameworks as a key source for protecting original exterior appearance in historic lilong neighborhoods [^9^]
- Cited in Gregory Bracken's *The Shanghai Lilong* (IIAS, 2020) [^14^]
- Referenced in the book *Aspects of Urbanization in China* (Amsterdam University Press, 2012), edited by Gregory Bracken [^15^]

### Conceptual Contribution
This paper establishes the theoretical foundation for understanding lilong as more than architectural heritage—it is a social-spatial concept. The redefinition of lilong as LMRHD housing has influenced subsequent urban design research on Shanghai. **Confidence: High**.

## Publication 4: "Gentrification from within" (2016) — 37 citations

### Bibliographic Details
- **Title:** Gentrification from within: urban social change as anthropological process
- **Journal:** Asian Anthropology, 15(1), 1-20
- **DOI:** 10.1080/1683478X.2016.1158227
- **Citations:** 37 (Google Scholar); 16 CrossRef citations

### Full Abstract
> "Based on ethnographic research in a traditional Shanghainese alleyway-house neighborhood (known locally as lilong) during 2013-2015, this study describes how knowledge of the global encourages pragmatic local residents to foresee a different future and voluntarily get involved in the process of urban renewal to enhance their own interests. This study unpacks the notion of architectural heritage as a selling point of dilapidated structures, which is the means through which local residents mobilize their knowledge to benefit themselves in the fight against local government authorities and the market economy. 'Gentrification from within' is the concept that I develop in this paper to explain this unique process of demographic change involving capital investment and cultural reproduction, in which the original residents themselves are the key actors in the diversification of a traditional neighborhood." [^16^]

### Key Arguments
1. **"Gentrification from within" as theoretical concept:** Original residents voluntarily participate in urban renewal to enhance their own interests
2. **Heritage as selling point:** Architectural heritage of dilapidated structures becomes a resource for residents
3. **Knowledge of the global:** Awareness of international heritage discourse enables residents to reframe their properties
4. **Resident-as-actor framework:** Original residents are key actors in neighborhood diversification, not passive victims

### Notable Scholarly Reception
- **Cited in The SAGE Handbook of Cultural Anthropology** (2021) in the chapter on Urban Anthropology [^17^]
- Cited in research on postcolonial gentrification in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, where the author notes that "this self-driven gentrification contributes to the complex historical trajectory of the city" [^18^]
- Referenced in a 4Cities European Master's thesis on urban heritage as anti-gentrification strategy, where the concept of "gentrification from within" is used analytically to describe how heritage building by local groups can inadvertently accelerate gentrification [^19^]
- Cited in research on investigating urban heritage as a strategy to counter gentrification in London [^19^]
- Referenced in the book *Housing as Heritage: The Great Urban Dilemma of the Global City of Shanghai* (Cambridge University Press) [^20^]
- Cited by Yujie Zhu and Placido Gonzalez Martinez in their work on heritage and governance in Chinese gentrification [^6^]
- Referenced in studies of student theses at Utrecht University on gentrification processes [^21^]

### Conceptual Contribution
This paper introduces Arkaraprasertkul's most significant theoretical contribution: the concept of "gentrification from within." This concept has been adopted by researchers studying gentrification across multiple contexts (China, Indonesia, UK, Greece) and represents a genuine theoretical innovation that challenges displacement-centric gentrification theory. The paper's influence extends beyond China studies into general gentrification theory. **Confidence: High** that this is his most theoretically generative paper.

## Publication 5: "Power, politics, and the making of Shanghai" (2010) — 32 citations

### Bibliographic Details
- **Title:** Power, politics, and the making of Shanghai
- **Journal:** Journal of Planning History, 9(4), 232-259
- **DOI:** 10.1177/1538513210382384
- **Citations:** 32 (Google Scholar)

### Full Abstract
> "How and why contemporary Shanghai has taken the form it has over the last thirty years and its role in the 'New' Shanghai through the underlying top-down governance and planning process? Answering this question, rather than taking solely the historical path in documenting the process of development, this article represents an attempt to make sense of the process of development through the underlying forces that shaped and reshaped the physical forms of the city. Particularly, this article looks at distinctive spatial forms and social and political formations associated with Shanghai before, under, and after the Mao Zedong. By weaving planning history with critical analysis of Chinese sociopolitics, this article chronologically traces the formative history of Shanghai's unique urbanism, building on existing literature and provides a wide coverage of the episodic accounts of Shanghai's planning history. When the role of individuals in the making of Shanghai becomes obvious especially after the reform in the early 1980s, this article pays attention to their roles in the development of Shanghai and the 'power and politics' they represented, which fostered the resurrection of the city." [^22^]

### Key Arguments
1. **Top-down governance analysis:** Shanghai's physical form results from underlying governance and planning processes
2. **Chronological tracing:** The article traces formative history through pre-Mao, Mao, and post-Mao periods
3. **Individual agency:** After 1980s reforms, individual actors played crucial roles in Shanghai's development
4. **Power and politics as shaping forces:** Urban form reflects sociopolitical power dynamics

### Notable Scholarly Reception
- **Cited in The Routledge Handbook of Planning Theory** (2017) as an authoritative study of Shanghai planning history [^23^]
- Listed in Cambridge University Press' Cambridge Urban History Biography as a source for Shanghai's urban history [^23^]
- Cited not only by area studies experts but also by cultural geographers, anthropologists, and historians of music
- Referenced in the book *Aspects of Urbanization in China* (Amsterdam University Press, 2012) [^15^]
- Cited in reviews of Qin Shao's *Shanghai Gone: Domicide and Defiance in a Chinese Megacity* [^24^]
- Referenced in a PhD thesis at Cardiff University on urban cultural history [^25^]

### Conceptual Contribution
This is Arkaraprasertkul's most historically ambitious paper, weaving planning history with critical sociopolitical analysis. It establishes the historical context that underpins his subsequent ethnographic work on gentrification and heritage. The paper's value lies in its coverage of Shanghai's planning history across the colonial, socialist, and reform eras. **Confidence: High**.

## Cross-Paper Analysis: Progression of Ideas

### Temporal Development of Arkaraprasertkul's Research Program

| Period | Paper | Focus | Theoretical Development |
|--------|-------|-------|------------------------|
| 2009 | Towards modern urban housing | Physical/architectural analysis of lilong | Establishes lilong as abstract social-spatial concept |
| 2010 | Power, politics, and the making of Shanghai | Historical/structural analysis | Provides macro-historical context for Shanghai's transformation |
| 2016 | Gentrification from within | Ethnographic micro-analysis | Introduces "gentrification from within" concept |
| 2018 | Gentrification and its contentment | Refined ethnographic analysis | Develops contentment/resident agency argument |
| 2019 | Gentrifying heritage | Heritage-gentrification nexus | Focuses on heritage as economic strategy |

### How the Papers Build on Each Other

1. **2009 → 2010: Architecture to Politics.** The 2009 paper's analysis of lilong as physical form is extended in 2010 into a political-historical analysis of how Shanghai's urban form was shaped by power structures.

2. **2010 → 2016: Structure to Agency.** The macro-historical analysis of state power in 2010 is complemented in 2016 by a focus on resident agency—the ability of ordinary Shanghainese to act within structural constraints.

3. **2016 → 2018: Concept to Evidence.** The 2018 paper provides the detailed ethnographic evidence (2013-2015 fieldwork) that supports and refines the "gentrification from within" concept introduced in 2016.

4. **2018 → 2019: Process to Heritage Mechanism.** The 2019 paper zooms in on the specific mechanism—heritage discourse—that enables gentrification from within.

### Unified Theoretical Framework

Across these five papers, Arkaraprasertkul constructs a coherent theoretical framework:
- **Level 1 (Structural):** Shanghai's urban form results from historical political processes (2010)
- **Level 2 (Typological):** The lilong represents a distinctive social-spatial formation (2009)
- **Level 3 (Processual):** Gentrification operates through resident agency, not just displacement (2016)
- **Level 4 (Experiential):** Residents experience gentrification through "contentment" and strategic action (2018)
- **Level 5 (Discursive):** Heritage discourse is the key tool enabling this alternative gentrification (2019)

## Assessment of Methodological Rigor

### Ethnographic Fieldwork Foundation

Arkaraprasertkul's most-cited papers are based on **intensive ethnographic fieldwork conducted during 2013-2015** in a Shanghai lilong neighborhood. This fieldwork involved:
- Living within the lilong community (participant observation) [^28^]
- Two years of sustained ethnographic engagement [^2^]
- Multiple interviews and informal conversations [^16^]

### Interdisciplinary Methodology

His approach weaves together:
1. **Architectural analysis:** Physical and spatial analysis of housing typologies
2. **Historical research:** Archival and documentary analysis of Shanghai's planning history
3. **Ethnographic fieldwork:** Participant observation and qualitative interviewing
4. **Political economy analysis:** Critical analysis of state-market-resident dynamics

This interdisciplinary methodology, combining his training as an architect with his doctoral work in anthropology, gives his work distinctive analytical power.

## Conclusions

Non Arkaraprasertkul's top five publications represent a remarkably coherent research program that advances from architectural typology analysis (2009) through historical political economy (2010) to ethnographic theory-building (2016, 2018, 2019). His most significant contribution is the concept of **"gentrification from within,"** which has been adopted by scholars across multiple continents and disciplines. The papers collectively establish him as a leading scholar of urban China whose work bridges architecture, anthropology, urban studies, and heritage studies. His endorsement by major scholars (Alan Smart, Fulong Wu), inclusion in major handbooks (SAGE, Routledge), adoption in Harvard curricula, and consistent citation in systematic reviews all confirm the foundational status of this body of work.
